# ASTROID-X — Computational Results Analysis

## 1. Introduction

This document presents the computational results obtained from the
ASTROID-X investigation of the astroid curve.

The purpose of the experimental phase was not simply to generate
visualizations, but to numerically verify mathematical properties
derived in the theoretical framework.

The investigation followed the principle:

> Theory → Derivation → Computation → Visualization → Validation → Interpretation

The experiments were organized into five major groups:

1. Geometric structure
2. Differential geometry
3. Numerical analysis
4. Singularity analysis
5. Generalization to a parameterized curve family

A total of eighteen computational experiments were performed,
identified as E01 through E18.

The standard astroid was represented primarily by the parametric
equations

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

and, where appropriate, by its implicit equation

\[
(x^2+y^2-a^2)^3+27a^2x^2y^2=0.
\]

Unless otherwise stated, numerical experiments used the scale
parameter

\[
a=5.
\]

The results below compare theoretical predictions with numerical
observations and identify the geometric meaning of the observed
behavior.
## 2. Experimental Methodology

The computational experiments were implemented in Python using
NumPy, Matplotlib, SciPy, and related numerical tools.

The experiments were designed so that mathematical quantities could
be calculated independently from the theoretical derivations and
then compared with known analytical results.

The main validation methods included:

- direct numerical comparison of equivalent representations,
- symmetry verification,
- numerical estimation of derivatives,
- curvature and radius-of-curvature calculations,
- convergence analysis,
- local log-log scaling,
- implicit-gradient evaluation,
- analytical area comparison,
- parameter-family comparison.

Numerical discrepancies were interpreted relative to the expected
floating-point precision of the computational environment.

Values close to machine precision were treated as numerical
round-off rather than mathematical disagreement.

For local singularity analysis, progressively smaller parameter
neighborhoods were used to determine whether numerical behavior
approached the theoretically predicted asymptotic form.
## 3. Geometric Results

### 3.1 E01 — Parametric Representation vs Cartesian Equation
### Objective

The first experiment tested whether the parametric representation of
the astroid produces points that satisfy its Cartesian equation.

The two representations are

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

and

\[
|x|^{2/3}+|y|^{2/3}=a^{2/3}.
\]

The purpose of this experiment was to verify computationally that
the parametric and Cartesian descriptions represent the same curve.

### Mathematical Expectation

Substituting the parametric equations into the Cartesian equation gives

\[
|a\cos^3(t)|^{2/3}
+
|a\sin^3(t)|^{2/3}.
\]

Since

\[
|\cos^3(t)|^{2/3}=\cos^2(t)
\]

and

\[
|\sin^3(t)|^{2/3}=\sin^2(t),
\]

the expression becomes

\[
a^{2/3}
\left(
\cos^2(t)+\sin^2(t)
\right).
\]

Using

\[
\cos^2(t)+\sin^2(t)=1,
\]

we obtain

\[
|x|^{2/3}+|y|^{2/3}=a^{2/3}.
\]

Therefore, every point generated by the parametric representation
should satisfy the Cartesian equation.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment provides a direct numerical consistency check between
the two representations.

A small numerical residual indicates that the difference between the
computed point and the expected Cartesian relation is caused only by
floating-point arithmetic.

The result supports the use of the parametric representation for
subsequent computational experiments because it provides a smooth
parameter-based description of the astroid away from its cusp
parameters.

### Research Significance

This verification establishes the computational foundation for the
remaining experiments.

The parametric representation can be used to calculate derivatives,
tangent directions, curvature, arc length, and local cusp behavior,
while the Cartesian and implicit representations provide independent
ways to validate the resulting geometry.
### 3.2 E02 — Symmetry Analysis
### Objective

The second experiment investigated the symmetry properties of the
astroid.

The standard astroid is expected to exhibit symmetry about both
coordinate axes and rotational symmetry of order four about the
origin.

The purpose of the experiment was to verify these properties
computationally from the parametric representation.

### Mathematical Basis

The astroid is given by

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t).
\]

Consider the transformation

\[
t\rightarrow -t.
\]

Since

\[
\cos(-t)=\cos(t)
\]

and

\[
\sin(-t)=-\sin(t),
\]

we obtain

\[
x(-t)=x(t),
\qquad
y(-t)=-y(t).
\]

Therefore, reflection across the \(x\)-axis leaves the curve
unchanged.

Similarly, using

\[
t\rightarrow \pi-t,
\]

we obtain

\[
x(\pi-t)=-x(t),
\qquad
y(\pi-t)=y(t),
\]

which demonstrates symmetry about the \(y\)-axis.

A rotation by \(\pi/2\) also maps the astroid onto itself, giving the
curve fourfold rotational symmetry about the origin.

### Computational Interpretation

The symmetry experiment compares corresponding points generated
under parameter transformations with their expected reflected or
rotated positions.

The numerical differences between the transformed points and the
theoretically expected points should remain at or near floating-point
precision.

This provides computational confirmation that the symmetry is not
merely a visual feature of the plotted curve, but follows directly
from the mathematical structure of its parametric representation.

### Research Significance

The symmetry of the astroid reduces the amount of independent
geometric analysis required.

Many properties can be investigated within one quadrant and then
extended to the remaining quadrants using symmetry.

This is particularly useful for:

- area calculations,
- arc-length calculations,
- curvature analysis,
- cusp analysis,
- numerical integration.

The symmetry therefore becomes both a geometric property and a
computational simplification.
### 3.3 E03 — Extrema and Coordinate Intercepts
### Objective

The third experiment investigated the extreme coordinate values and
axis intercepts of the astroid.

For the standard astroid

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the expected coordinate extrema are

\[
-a\leq x\leq a,
\qquad
-a\leq y\leq a.
\]

The corresponding extreme points are expected to occur at the four
cusps.

### Mathematical Basis

Since

\[
-1\leq\cos(t)\leq1,
\]

we have

\[
-a\leq a\cos^3(t)\leq a.
\]

Therefore,

\[
x_{\max}=a,
\qquad
x_{\min}=-a.
\]

Similarly,

\[
y_{\max}=a,
\qquad
y_{\min}=-a.
\]

The four extreme points are therefore

\[
(a,0),\qquad
(0,a),\qquad
(-a,0),\qquad
(0,-a).
\]

These points are simultaneously the coordinate-axis intercepts and
the cusp locations of the astroid.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment numerically sampled the parametric curve and
determined its minimum and maximum \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates.

For the standard experimental value

\[
a=5,
\]

the expected coordinate ranges are

\[
-5\leq x\leq5,
\qquad
-5\leq y\leq5.
\]

The numerical extrema should approach these theoretical values,
with any small discrepancy resulting from the finite parameter
sampling.

### Research Significance

The extrema provide an important geometric scale for the astroid.

They establish that the curve is bounded inside the square

\[
[-a,a]\times[-a,a]
\]

and that its four axis intercepts correspond exactly to its four
cusps.

This result is also useful for later numerical experiments because
the coordinate bounds provide a simple consistency check for generated
data.
## 4. Differential-Geometric Results

### 4.1 E04 — Tangent and Slope Behavior
### Objective

The fourth experiment investigated the tangent direction and slope
of the astroid at different parameter values.

For the parametric representation

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the tangent direction is determined by the derivative

\[
\mathbf{r}'(t)
=
\left(
\frac{dx}{dt},
\frac{dy}{dt}
\right).
\]

The purpose of this experiment was to verify the theoretical slope
of the astroid and investigate how the tangent direction changes
around the curve.

### Mathematical Basis

Differentiating the parametric equations gives

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}
=
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
\]

and

\[
\frac{dy}{dt}
=
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t).
\]

For a regular point where

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}\neq0,
\]

the slope of the tangent is

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}.
\]

Substituting the derivatives gives

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t)
}{
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
}.
\]

After simplification,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\tan(t)
}
\]

for the smooth portions of the curve.

This result shows that the tangent slope is directly controlled by
the parameter \(t\).

### Tangent Direction

The tangent vector is

\[
\mathbf{r}'(t)
=
\left(
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t),
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t)
\right).
\]

Its direction changes continuously along each smooth portion of the
astroid.

For example:

- Near \(t=0\), the tangent approaches a horizontal direction.
- Near \(t=\pi/4\), the tangent has slope \(-1\).
- Near \(t=\pi/2\), the tangent approaches a vertical direction.
- The same behavior repeats in the other quadrants because of the
  symmetry of the astroid.

### Behavior at the Cusps

At the four cusp parameters

\[
t=0,\quad
\frac{\pi}{2},\quad
\pi,\quad
\frac{3\pi}{2},
\]

the derivative satisfies

\[
\mathbf{r}'(t)=\mathbf{0}.
\]

Therefore, the usual regular-curve tangent formula cannot be
applied directly at the cusps.

However, the limiting tangent direction can still be observed.

For example, near the cusp

\[
(a,0),
\]

the slope approaches

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}\rightarrow0,
\]

so the limiting tangent direction is horizontal.

This distinction is important because the curve has a definite
limiting tangent direction at the cusp even though its parametric
velocity vanishes there.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment evaluates the derivatives numerically over a dense
set of parameter values.

The calculated tangent slope is compared with the theoretical
expression

\[
-\tan(t).
\]

Agreement between the two expressions provides a numerical
verification of the differential structure of the astroid.

The cusp parameters are treated separately because the tangent vector
vanishes at those points.

### Research Significance

The tangent analysis provides the foundation for several later
experiments in ASTROID-X.

In particular, the tangent vector is required for studying:

- normal directions,
- curvature,
- radius of curvature,
- evolute behavior,
- local cusp geometry.

Therefore, E04 establishes the first major connection between the
parametric representation and the differential geometry of the
astroid.
### 4.2 E05 — Concavity Analysis
### Objective

The fifth experiment investigated the concavity of the astroid and
how its curvature as a graph changes across different regions of the
curve.

For the first-quadrant portion of the astroid, the curve can be
considered as a function \(y(x)\). The objective is to determine the
sign of the second derivative

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
\]

and verify the result computationally.

### Mathematical Basis

From E04, the slope of the astroid is

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\tan(t).
\]

To obtain the second derivative, differentiate with respect to the
parameter \(t\):

\[
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
\frac{dy}{dx}
\right)
=
-\sec^2(t).
\]

Using

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
=
\frac{
d(dy/dx)/dt
}{
dx/dt
},
\]

and

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}
=
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t),
\]

we obtain

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
=
\frac{
-\sec^2(t)
}{
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
}.
\]

Since

\[
\sec^2(t)=\frac{1}{\cos^2(t)},
\]

this simplifies to

\[
\boxed{
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
=
\frac{1}{
3a\cos^4(t)\sin(t)
}
}
\]

for the first quadrant.

### Concavity in the First Quadrant

For

\[
0<t<\frac{\pi}{2},
\]

we have

\[
a>0,\qquad
\cos^4(t)>0,\qquad
\sin(t)>0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}>0.
\]

Hence the first-quadrant portion of the astroid is

\[
\boxed{\text{concave upward}}
\]

when represented as \(y(x)\).

The magnitude of the second derivative increases strongly near the
cusps because the denominator approaches zero.

### Behavior Near the Cusps

The expression for the second derivative becomes singular as the
parameter approaches the cusp values.

For example, near

\[
t\rightarrow0,
\]

we have

\[
\sin(t)\rightarrow0
\]

and therefore

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\rightarrow\infty.
\]

This does not mean that the curve itself becomes undefined.
Instead, it indicates increasingly rapid variation of the graph's
slope as the cusp is approached.

This behavior is consistent with the local cusp analysis performed
in E12 and E13.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment calculates the numerical first and second derivatives
of the parametric curve.

The numerical second derivative is then compared with the theoretical
expression

\[
\frac{1}{
3a\cos^4(t)\sin(t)
}.
\]

Away from the cusp points, the numerical and theoretical results
should agree closely.

Very close to a cusp, numerical errors can become larger because the
derivative magnitude becomes very large.

Therefore, points extremely close to the cusps should be interpreted
carefully.

### Important Geometric Distinction

Graph concavity should not be confused with the global geometric
properties of the astroid.

The statement

\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}>0
\]

describes the concavity of a particular portion of the curve when it
is represented as a function \(y(x)\).

It does not by itself provide a complete description of the global
geometry of the closed astroid, especially because the curve contains
four cusps where the regularity conditions fail.

### Research Significance

The concavity analysis demonstrates how the local shape of the
astroid can be obtained directly from its parametric representation.

It also establishes an important connection between:

\[
\text{parameterization}
\rightarrow
\text{slope}
\rightarrow
\text{second derivative}
\rightarrow
\text{local shape}.
\]

The increasing magnitude of the second derivative near the cusps
provides another computational indication of the special local
geometry already observed in E12 and E13.
### 4.3 E06 — Curvature Analysis
### Objective

The sixth experiment investigated the curvature of the astroid.

While E04 studied the direction of the tangent and E05 studied
concavity, curvature provides a quantitative measure of how rapidly
the tangent direction changes along the curve.

For the parametric astroid

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the objective is to derive the curvature function and verify its
behavior computationally.

### Mathematical Basis

For a parametric plane curve

\[
\mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)),
\]

the curvature is given by

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{
|x'y''-y'x''|
}{
\left(x'^2+y'^2\right)^{3/2}
}.
\]

For the astroid,

\[
x'= -3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
\]

and

\[
y'=3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t).
\]

Differentiating again gives

\[
x''
=
3a\left(
2\cos(t)\sin^2(t)-\cos^3(t)
\right)
\]

and

\[
y''
=
3a\left(
2\sin(t)\cos^2(t)-\sin^3(t)
\right).
\]

Substituting these derivatives into the curvature formula and
simplifying gives

\[
\boxed{
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{1}{
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
}
}
\]

on the smooth portions of the astroid.

Using

\[
|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
=
\frac{1}{2}|\sin(2t)|,
\]

the curvature can also be written as

\[
\boxed{
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{2}{
3a|\sin(2t)|
}
}.
\]

### Interpretation of the Curvature Function

The curvature depends strongly on the parameter \(t\).

At

\[
t=\frac{\pi}{4},
\]

we have

\[
|\sin(t)\cos(t)|=\frac12.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\kappa_{\min}
=
\frac{2}{3a}.
\]

Thus, the smallest curvature occurs halfway between the axis
directions in each quadrant.

For the experimental value

\[
a=5,
\]

the minimum curvature is

\[
\kappa_{\min}
=
\frac{2}{15}
\approx0.1333.
\]

### Behavior Near the Cusps

At the cusp parameters

\[
t=0,\quad
\frac{\pi}{2},\quad
\pi,\quad
\frac{3\pi}{2},
\]

we have

\[
|\sin(t)\cos(t)|\rightarrow0.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
\kappa(t)\rightarrow\infty
}
\]

as a cusp is approached.

This shows mathematically that the astroid bends increasingly sharply
near its cusps.

The curvature is therefore not finite at the cusps themselves,
because the curve is not regular there.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment evaluates the curvature formula numerically over a
dense set of parameter values.

The numerical results can be visualized as a function

\[
\kappa(t)
\]

to observe:

1. minimum curvature between the cusps,
2. rapid growth of curvature near each cusp,
3. fourfold repetition caused by the symmetry of the astroid.

Points extremely close to the cusps require careful numerical
handling because the theoretical curvature becomes very large.

### Research Significance

The curvature experiment provides a quantitative description of the
local bending of the astroid.

It connects the earlier differential results:

\[
\text{position}
\rightarrow
\text{tangent}
\rightarrow
\text{slope}
\rightarrow
\text{concavity}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}.
\]

The divergence of curvature near the cusps also provides an
independent computational signature of the singular behavior studied
in E12, E13, and E16.

This makes curvature an important bridge between the differential
geometry and singularity analysis of ASTROID-X.
### 4.4 E07 — Curvature Variation
### Objective

The seventh experiment investigated how the curvature of the astroid
varies as a point moves around the curve.

E06 established the curvature function

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{1}{
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
}.
\]

The purpose of E07 is to study the variation of this quantity rather
than only its formula.

The experiment focuses on identifying regions of low and high
curvature and determining how the fourfold symmetry of the astroid
appears in the curvature distribution.

### Mathematical Basis

The curvature can be written as

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{2}{
3a|\sin(2t)|
}.
\]

Because the denominator contains

\[
|\sin(2t)|,
\]

the curvature repeats periodically as the parameter moves around the
astroid.

The curvature is smallest when

\[
|\sin(2t)|=1.
\]

This occurs at

\[
t=
\frac{\pi}{4},
\frac{3\pi}{4},
\frac{5\pi}{4},
\frac{7\pi}{4}.
\]

At these points,

\[
\kappa_{\min}
=
\frac{2}{3a}.
\]

For \(a=5\),

\[
\kappa_{\min}
=
\frac{2}{15}
\approx0.133333.
\]

### Curvature Growth

As the parameter approaches any cusp,

\[
t\rightarrow
0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2},
\]

we have

\[
|\sin(2t)|\rightarrow0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\kappa(t)\rightarrow\infty.
\]

This means that the curvature becomes increasingly large near the
cusps.

The curvature profile therefore has four repeating high-curvature
regions corresponding to the four cusps and four low-curvature
regions between them.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment evaluates

\[
\kappa(t)
\]

over a dense parameter grid and plots the resulting curvature
profile.

The expected numerical pattern is:

- low curvature near the middle of each quadrant,
- increasing curvature as the curve approaches a cusp,
- very large curvature close to the cusp parameters,
- fourfold repetition around the complete curve.

The numerical experiment should reproduce this theoretical
structure.

Very close to the cusp parameters, the curvature can become
extremely large. These points should therefore be handled carefully
when plotting or performing numerical calculations.

### Geometric Interpretation

Curvature measures how rapidly the tangent direction changes as a
point moves along the curve.

Consequently:

\[
\text{small curvature}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{gentler bending},
\]

while

\[
\text{large curvature}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{sharper bending}.
\]

For the astroid, the regions between the cusps therefore represent
the relatively gently bending portions of the curve, while the
neighborhoods of the cusps exhibit increasingly sharp local
behavior.

This provides a geometric interpretation of the curvature function
rather than treating it only as an algebraic quantity.

### Symmetry and Curvature

The astroid has fourfold rotational symmetry.

The curvature inherits this symmetry because

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{2}{
3a|\sin(2t)|
}.
\]

A rotation by

\[
\frac{\pi}{2}
\]

corresponds to shifting the parameter by \(\pi/2\), which leaves the
absolute value of \(\sin(2t)\) unchanged.

Therefore, the curvature distribution repeats identically in all
four quadrants.

### Research Significance

E07 extends the curvature analysis from a single formula to a global
description of how bending is distributed around the astroid.

The experiment demonstrates that the singular geometry of the cusps
is reflected directly in the curvature profile.

This establishes the relationship

\[
\text{global symmetry}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature distribution}
\rightarrow
\text{local cusp behavior}.
\]

The curvature variation will also be useful for interpreting the
radius of curvature and evolute experiments that follow.
### 4.5 E08 — Radius of Curvature
### Objective

The eighth experiment investigated the radius of curvature of the
astroid.

The curvature studied in E06 and E07 measures how strongly the curve
bends at a given point. The radius of curvature provides the
corresponding geometric length scale.

The relationship between curvature and radius of curvature is

\[
\rho(t)=\frac{1}{\kappa(t)}.
\]

The purpose of E08 is to determine this quantity for the astroid and
study how it changes around the curve.

### Mathematical Basis

From E06, the curvature of the astroid is

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{1}{
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
}.
\]

Therefore, the radius of curvature is

\[
\rho(t)
=
\frac{1}{\kappa(t)}.
\]

Substituting the curvature expression gives

\[
\boxed{
\rho(t)
=
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
}
\]

or equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\rho(t)
=
\frac{3a}{2}|\sin(2t)|
}.
\]

### Maximum Radius of Curvature

The radius of curvature is largest when

\[
|\sin(2t)|=1.
\]

This occurs at

\[
t=
\frac{\pi}{4},
\frac{3\pi}{4},
\frac{5\pi}{4},
\frac{7\pi}{4}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\rho_{\max}
=
\frac{3a}{2}.
\]

For the experimental value

\[
a=5,
\]

the maximum radius of curvature is

\[
\rho_{\max}
=
\frac{15}{2}
=
7.5.
\]

These locations correspond to the regions halfway between the
coordinate-axis cusps.

### Behavior Near the Cusps

At the cusp parameters,

\[
t=
0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2},
\]

we have

\[
|\sin(t)\cos(t)|=0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{
\rho(t)\rightarrow0
}
\]

as a cusp is approached.

This is the inverse behavior of curvature.

Since

\[
\kappa(t)\rightarrow\infty,
\]

we have

\[
\rho(t)=\frac{1}{\kappa(t)}
\rightarrow0.
\]

Thus, the radius of curvature provides another quantitative
description of the increasingly sharp geometry near the cusps.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment evaluates the radius of curvature numerically using

\[
\rho(t)
=
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
\]

and compares it with the corresponding curvature obtained in E06.

The expected numerical behavior is:

- radius of curvature approaches zero near each cusp,
- radius reaches its maximum between consecutive cusps,
- the pattern repeats four times around the curve,
- the radius and curvature exhibit inverse behavior.

The numerical results should agree with the theoretical expression up
to floating-point precision.

### Relationship with Curvature

The two quantities provide complementary descriptions of the same
local geometry:

\[
\kappa(t)=\frac{1}{\rho(t)}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\text{large curvature}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{small radius of curvature},
\]

and

\[
\text{small curvature}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{large radius of curvature}.
\]

For the astroid, this relationship is especially clear near the
cusps, where the curvature becomes unbounded while the radius of
curvature approaches zero.

### Research Significance

E08 converts the curvature analysis into a geometric length scale.

Instead of describing the bending only through the value of
\(\kappa\), the radius of curvature indicates the size of the
osculating circle associated with the local geometry.

The result also prepares the mathematical framework for E09, where
the centers of these local curvature circles are investigated through
the evolute of the astroid.

Thus, the progression becomes

\[
\text{curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{radius of curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{center of curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{evolute}.
\]
### 4.6 E09 — Evolute Analysis
### Objective

The ninth experiment investigated the evolute of the astroid.

The evolute is the locus of the centers of curvature of a curve.
Therefore, it provides a geometric representation of how the local
curvature changes around the original curve.

The purpose of this experiment was to:

- derive the center of curvature,
- generate the evolute parametrically,
- visualize its relationship with the astroid,
- investigate its symmetry and behavior near the cusps.

### Mathematical Basis

The astroid is given by

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t).
\]

From the previous experiments, the derivatives are

\[
x'=-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
\]

and

\[
y'=3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t).
\]

The curvature is

\[
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{1}{
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|
}.
\]

The radius of curvature is therefore

\[
\rho(t)
=
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|.
\]

### Center of Curvature

For a parametric curve, the center of curvature can be obtained by
moving from the point on the curve in the principal normal direction
by the radius of curvature.

For the astroid, simplifying this expression gives the center of
curvature

\[
\boxed{
X(t)
=
a\cos^3(t)
-
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t)
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
Y(t)
=
a\sin^3(t)
-
3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
}.
\]

These expressions can be factorized as

\[
X(t)
=
a\cos(t)
\left(
\cos^2(t)-3\sin^2(t)
\right)
\]

and

\[
Y(t)
=
a\sin(t)
\left(
\sin^2(t)-3\cos^2(t)
\right).
\]

Using

\[
\sin^2(t)+\cos^2(t)=1,
\]

they can also be written as

\[
X(t)
=
a\cos(t)
\left(
1-4\sin^2(t)
\right)
\]

and

\[
Y(t)
=
a\sin(t)
\left(
1-4\cos^2(t)
\right).
\]

The curve traced by

\[
(X(t),Y(t))
\]

is the evolute of the astroid.

### Behavior at the Cusps

As the parameter approaches a cusp,

\[
t\rightarrow0,
\]

the radius of curvature satisfies

\[
\rho(t)\rightarrow0.
\]

Therefore, the center of curvature approaches the corresponding
point of the original astroid.

For example,

\[
t\rightarrow0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
(X,Y)\rightarrow(a,0).
\]

Similarly, the other cusp limits are

\[
(0,a),\qquad
(-a,0),\qquad
(0,-a).
\]

Thus, the evolute connects to the original curve at the cusp
locations in the limiting sense.

### Symmetry

The center-of-curvature equations inherit the fourfold rotational
symmetry of the astroid.

Consequently, the evolute also exhibits rotational symmetry about the
origin.

This allows the behavior observed in one quadrant to be related to
the corresponding behavior in the other three quadrants.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment generates a dense set of parameter values and
calculates both:

\[
(x(t),y(t))
\]

for the original astroid and

\[
(X(t),Y(t))
\]

for its centers of curvature.

Both curves are then plotted together.

The visualization allows the following features to be investigated:

- the location of the centers of curvature,
- the symmetry of the evolute,
- the movement of the centers as the parameter changes,
- the approach of the centers toward the cusp locations.

The numerical calculation should reproduce the theoretical
parametric equations of the evolute.

### Geometric Interpretation

The astroid and its evolute provide two related descriptions of the
same differential geometry.

The astroid describes the original position of the curve, while the
evolute records the locations of its local centers of curvature.

Therefore,

\[
\text{astroid}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{radius of curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{center of curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{evolute}.
\]

The evolute is consequently a higher-level geometric representation
of the curvature structure investigated in E06–E08.

### Research Significance

E09 completes the main differential-geometric sequence of
ASTROID-X.

The experiments E04–E09 now form a continuous chain:

\[
\text{tangent}
\rightarrow
\text{concavity}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature variation}
\rightarrow
\text{radius of curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{evolute}.
\]

This sequence demonstrates how increasingly detailed geometric
information can be extracted from the same parametric representation.

The evolute also provides a useful visualization of the local
geometry that cannot be obtained from the original curve alone.
## 5. Numerical Validation Results
### 5.1 E10 — Arc-Length Numerical Convergence

### Objective

The tenth experiment numerically estimated the perimeter of the
astroid and compared the result with its known analytical value.

For the standard astroid,

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the theoretical perimeter is

\[
\boxed{L=6a}.
\]

The purpose of E10 was to determine whether numerical integration of
the parametric curve converges toward this analytical result as the
number of sampling points increases.

### Mathematical Basis

For a parametric curve, the arc length is

\[
L=
\int
\sqrt{
\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2
}
\,dt.
\]

For the astroid,

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}
=
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
\]

and

\[
\frac{dy}{dt}
=
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t).
\]

Therefore, the speed is

\[
\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}
=
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|.
\]

Hence,

\[
L
=
\int_0^{2\pi}
3a|\sin(t)\cos(t)|\,dt.
\]

Using symmetry,

\[
L
=
4
\int_0^{\pi/2}
3a\sin(t)\cos(t)\,dt.
\]

Since

\[
\int_0^{\pi/2}
\sin(t)\cos(t)\,dt
=
\frac12,
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{L=6a}.
\]

For

\[
a=5,
\]

the expected perimeter is

\[
L=30.
\]

### Numerical Method

The experiment approximates the arc length using increasingly dense
parameter grids.

For consecutive sampled points

\[
(x_i,y_i)
\]

and

\[
(x_{i+1},y_{i+1}),
\]

the local curve segment is approximated by the Euclidean distance

\[
\Delta L_i
=
\sqrt{
(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2+
(y_{i+1}-y_i)^2
}.
\]

The total numerical perimeter is then

\[
L_n
=
\sum_i \Delta L_i.
\]

The value \(L_n\) is calculated for several sampling resolutions.

### Convergence Interpretation

For a sufficiently fine parameter grid, the polygonal approximation
should approach the actual arc length.

Therefore,

\[
L_n\rightarrow6a
\]

as the number of sampling points increases.

For the experimental value \(a=5\),

\[
L_n\rightarrow30.
\]

The convergence behavior provides a direct numerical validation of
the analytical perimeter formula.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment does not simply calculate a single perimeter value.

Instead, it studies the relationship between:

\[
\text{sampling resolution}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{numerical error}.
\]

The numerical error can be defined as

\[
E_n
=
|L_n-6a|.
\]

As the sampling resolution increases, the expected behavior is

\[
E_n\rightarrow0.
\]

This demonstrates that the computational approximation becomes more
accurate as the discretization is refined.

### Research Significance

E10 connects the analytical geometry of the astroid with numerical
approximation.

The experiment demonstrates that a known geometric quantity can be
recovered computationally without directly using its closed-form
value.

The convergence study also establishes a general numerical principle
used throughout ASTROID-X:

\[
\boxed{
\text{increase resolution}
\rightarrow
\text{reduce discretization error}
\rightarrow
\text{approach theoretical result}
}
\]

This provides an important validation framework for later numerical
experiments involving area and generalized curves.
### 5.2 E11 — Area Numerical Convergence
### Objective

The eleventh experiment numerically estimated the area enclosed by
the astroid and compared it with the known analytical result.

For the standard astroid,

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the theoretical enclosed area is

\[
\boxed{
A=\frac{3\pi a^2}{8}
}.
\]

The purpose of E11 was to determine whether numerical integration
converges toward this analytical value as the sampling resolution is
increased.

### Mathematical Basis

For a closed parametric curve, the enclosed area can be calculated
using

\[
A
=
\frac12
\left|
\int_0^{2\pi}
\left(
x\frac{dy}{dt}
-
y\frac{dx}{dt}
\right)
dt
\right|.
\]

For the astroid,

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

with

\[
x'=-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t)
\]

and

\[
y'=3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t).
\]

Substituting these expressions gives

\[
xy'-yx'
=
3a^2\cos^2(t)\sin^2(t).
\]

Therefore,

\[
A
=
\frac32a^2
\int_0^{2\pi}
\cos^2(t)\sin^2(t)\,dt.
\]

Using

\[
\cos^2(t)\sin^2(t)
=
\frac14\sin^2(2t),
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{
A=\frac{3\pi a^2}{8}
}.
\]

For the experimental value

\[
a=5,
\]

the theoretical area is

\[
A
=
\frac{3\pi(25)}{8}
\approx29.452431.
\]

### Numerical Method

The experiment approximates the area using discretized values of the
parametric curve.

The parameter interval

\[
0\leq t\leq2\pi
\]

is divided into increasingly fine grids.

The numerical integral is then evaluated using the sampled values of

\[
x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t).
\]

The resulting numerical area is compared with

\[
A_{\text{theoretical}}
=
\frac{3\pi a^2}{8}.
\]

### Convergence Analysis

For a numerical approximation using \(n\) sampling points, define the
absolute error as

\[
E_n
=
|A_n-A_{\text{theoretical}}|.
\]

As the resolution increases, the expected behavior is

\[
A_n
\rightarrow
A_{\text{theoretical}}
\]

and therefore

\[
E_n\rightarrow0.
\]

This provides a numerical convergence test for the analytical area
formula.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment studies not only the final numerical area but also
how the approximation changes with resolution.

A convergence plot can be used to visualize

\[
n
\quad\text{versus}\quad
A_n
\]

and

\[
n
\quad\text{versus}\quad
E_n.
\]

If the numerical method is behaving correctly, the calculated area
should approach

\[
29.452431
\]

for \(a=5\).

The decreasing error demonstrates that the difference between the
numerical and analytical values is primarily a discretization effect.

### Research Significance

E11 provides an independent computational validation of one of the
most important global geometric properties of the astroid: its
enclosed area.

Together, E10 and E11 demonstrate that numerical discretization can
recover both:

\[
\text{perimeter}
\]

and

\[
\text{area}
\]

from the parametric representation.

This strengthens the computational validation framework of
ASTROID-X by connecting analytical formulas with numerical
convergence experiments.
## 6. Singularity Analysis Results
### 6.1 E12 — Cusp Local Behavior
### Objective

The twelfth experiment investigated the local behavior of the astroid
near one of its cusp points.

The cusp selected for analysis is

\[
(a,0).
\]

The purpose of the experiment was to determine whether the numerical
curve near this point follows the theoretical semicubical cusp
behavior derived from the parametric representation.

### Mathematical Basis

The astroid is

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t).
\]

Near the cusp \((a,0)\), the parameter satisfies

\[
t\rightarrow0.
\]

Using the small-angle approximations

\[
\cos(t)\approx1-\frac{t^2}{2}
\]

and

\[
\sin(t)\approx t,
\]

we obtain

\[
x
\approx
a\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2}\right)^3.
\]

Keeping the leading non-zero correction gives

\[
x\approx
a-\frac{3a}{2}t^2.
\]

Similarly,

\[
y
\approx
at^3.
\]

Therefore,

\[
a-x
\approx
\frac{3a}{2}t^2.
\]

Eliminating \(t\) gives

\[
\boxed{
y^2
\sim
\frac{8}{27a}(a-x)^3
}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
|y|
\sim
\sqrt{\frac{8}{27a}}
(a-x)^{3/2}
}.
\]

This is the characteristic local scaling of a semicubical cusp.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment compared the actual astroid near \((a,0)\) with the
local approximation

\[
|y|
=
\sqrt{\frac{8}{27a}}
(a-x)^{3/2}.
\]

For

\[
a=5,
\]

the numerical experiment showed that the local approximation follows
the astroid increasingly closely as the neighborhood becomes smaller.

The experiment also estimated the exponent from a log-log
relationship between

\[
a-x
\]

and

\[
|y|.
\]

The initial estimate was approximately

\[
1.511091,
\]

compared with the theoretical value

\[
1.5.
\]

### Research Interpretation

The small difference between the numerical estimate and the
theoretical exponent is caused by using a finite neighborhood rather
than the exact limiting process.

The result nevertheless provides strong numerical evidence for the
predicted cusp scaling.

The experiment establishes that the cusp is not merely a visual
sharp point. It has a specific mathematical local structure described
by the power law

\[
|y|\propto(a-x)^{3/2}.
\]

This provides the foundation for the convergence study in E13.
### 6.2 E13 — Cusp Scaling and Convergence
### Objective

The thirteenth experiment investigated whether the numerically
estimated cusp exponent converges toward its theoretical value as the
neighborhood around the cusp becomes progressively smaller.

From E12, the theoretical local behavior is

\[
|y|
\sim
C(a-x)^{3/2},
\]

where

\[
C=\sqrt{\frac{8}{27a}}.
\]

Therefore, the expected scaling exponent is

\[
\boxed{\frac32=1.5}.
\]

### Numerical Method

The experiment considered several increasingly small parameter
neighborhoods around

\[
t=0.
\]

For each maximum parameter value \(t_{\max}\), the relationship

\[
\log|y|
\]

versus

\[
\log(a-x)
\]

was fitted using a linear model.

From

\[
|y|\sim C(a-x)^{3/2},
\]

taking logarithms gives

\[
\log|y|
\sim
\log C+
\frac32\log(a-x).
\]

Therefore, the slope of the log-log fit provides an estimate of the
cusp exponent.

### Numerical Results

For the astroid with

\[
a=5,
\]

the experiment produced the following results:

| Maximum \(|t|\) | Estimated Exponent | Relative Error |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.500 | 1.510479 | 0.6986% |
| 0.250 | 1.502631 | 0.1754% |
| 0.100 | 1.500421 | 0.0281% |
| 0.050 | 1.500111 | 0.0074% |
| 0.025 | 1.500028 | 0.0019% |

The theoretical exponent is

\[
1.5.
\]

### Convergence Behavior

The numerical results show clear convergence toward the theoretical
value.

The estimated exponent changes from

\[
1.510479
\]

for the largest neighborhood to

\[
1.500028
\]

for the smallest tested neighborhood.

The final relative error is approximately

\[
0.0019\%.
\]

This demonstrates that reducing the neighborhood around the cusp
significantly improves the numerical estimation of the local scaling
law.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment demonstrates an important principle in local numerical
analysis.

The theoretical cusp exponent describes limiting behavior:

\[
t\rightarrow0.
\]

A numerical experiment cannot directly evaluate an infinitely small
neighborhood, so progressively smaller finite neighborhoods are used
instead.

The convergence of the estimated exponent toward

\[
\frac32
\]

provides numerical evidence that the predicted local asymptotic
behavior is correct.

### Research Significance

E13 provides stronger evidence for the cusp result than a single
numerical estimate.

Rather than obtaining a value close to \(1.5\) only once, the
experiment demonstrates systematic convergence toward \(1.5\).

The result therefore establishes the chain

\[
\text{local theoretical model}
\rightarrow
\text{numerical estimation}
\rightarrow
\text{zoom refinement}
\rightarrow
\text{convergence}.
\]

This makes E13 an important validation experiment within the
singularity analysis of ASTROID-X.
### 6.3 E14 — Radial Distance Behavior
### Objective

The fourteenth experiment investigated how the radial distance from
the origin changes as a point moves around the astroid.

For the parametric representation

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

the radial distance is

\[
r(t)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
\]

The purpose of E14 was to derive a simplified expression for this
distance and verify its minimum and maximum values numerically.

### Mathematical Basis

Substituting the parametric equations gives

\[
r(t)
=
a\sqrt{\cos^6(t)+\sin^6(t)}.
\]

Using

\[
\cos^6(t)+\sin^6(t)
=
(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)^3
-
3\sin^2t\cos^2t,
\]

and

\[
\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1,
\]

we obtain

\[
r(t)
=
a\sqrt{
1-3\sin^2(t)\cos^2(t)
}.
\]

Since

\[
\sin^2(t)\cos^2(t)
=
\frac14\sin^2(2t),
\]

the radial distance becomes

\[
\boxed{
r(t)
=
a\sqrt{
1-\frac34\sin^2(2t)
}
}.
\]

### Minimum and Maximum Radial Distance

The maximum radial distance occurs when

\[
\sin^2(2t)=0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{r_{\max}=a}.
\]

These values occur at the four cusp directions.

The minimum radial distance occurs when

\[
\sin^2(2t)=1.
\]

Therefore,

\[
r_{\min}
=
a\sqrt{1-\frac34}
=
\boxed{\frac a2}.
\]

For

\[
a=5,
\]

the theoretical values are

\[
r_{\min}=2.5
\]

and

\[
r_{\max}=5.
\]

### Numerical Results

The numerical experiment produced

\[
r_{\min}\approx2.500002
\]

and

\[
r_{\max}=5.000000.
\]

The theoretical values are

\[
r_{\min}=2.500000
\]

and

\[
r_{\max}=5.000000.
\]

The maximum difference between the numerical and theoretical radial
expressions was approximately

\[
1.78\times10^{-15},
\]

which is effectively zero at ordinary floating-point precision.

The numerical minimum occurred near

\[
t\approx0.785791,
\]

which is very close to

\[
\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment provides two independent descriptions of the radial
distance:

1. Directly calculating

\[
r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
\]

from the sampled coordinates.

2. Evaluating the derived expression

\[
r(t)
=
a\sqrt{
1-\frac34\sin^2(2t)
}.
\]

The extremely small difference between these two calculations
provides numerical confirmation of the derived radial formula.

### Geometric Interpretation

For the standard astroid, the radial distance is bounded by

\[
\boxed{
\frac a2\leq r\leq a
}.
\]

Therefore, the entire astroid lies outside the circle

\[
r=\frac a2
\]

and inside the circle

\[
r=a.
\]

For \(a=5\), these bounding circles have radii

\[
2.5
\]

and

\[
5.

### Research Significance

E14 provides a radial description of the astroid that complements
the Cartesian and parametric analyses.

It establishes another quantitative geometric constraint and
demonstrates how a complicated-looking coordinate expression can be
reduced to a simple periodic radial function.

The experiment also provides an additional independent validation
of the computational implementation used throughout ASTROID-X.
### 6.4 E15 — Normal Behavior
### Objective

The fifteenth experiment investigated the normal direction of the
astroid and numerically verified that the tangent and normal
directions are perpendicular.

The experiment builds on the tangent analysis from E04 and provides
the geometric normal field required for understanding curvature and
the evolute.

### Mathematical Basis

The tangent vector of the astroid is

\[
\mathbf r'(t)
=
\left(
-3a\cos^2(t)\sin(t),
3a\sin^2(t)\cos(t)
\right).
\]

Away from the cusp points, the corresponding unit tangent vector can
be obtained by normalizing this derivative.

A convenient unit normal direction for the astroid is

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf N(t)
=
(\sin t,\cos t)
}
\]

up to orientation.

The length of this vector is

\[
\|\mathbf N(t)\|
=
\sqrt{\sin^2(t)+\cos^2(t)}
=
1.
\]

Therefore, it is a unit vector.

### Perpendicularity

The numerical experiment calculates the unit tangent

\[
\mathbf T(t)
\]

and the normal

\[
\mathbf N(t).
\]

Their dot product should satisfy

\[
\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf N=0.
\]

Thus,

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf N=0
}
\]

provides the perpendicularity condition.

The exact cusp parameters are excluded because

\[
\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf0
\]

there, so a unit tangent cannot be defined directly.

### Numerical Results

For

\[
a=5,
\]

the experiment produced

\[
\max|\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf N|
\approx
1.665335\times10^{-16}.
\]

The theoretical value is

\[
0.
\]

The normal-vector length was also verified numerically, with

\[
\max\left|
\|\mathbf N\|-1
\right|
\approx
1.110223\times10^{-16}.
\]

Both errors are at the scale of floating-point precision.

### Computational Interpretation

The experiment therefore confirms two properties:

\[
\|\mathbf N\|=1
\]

and

\[
\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf N=0.
\]

The graphical normal field also shows how the normal direction changes
continuously along the smooth portions of the astroid.

The numerical results demonstrate that the calculated normal vectors
are consistent with the theoretical geometry.

### Relationship with Curvature

The normal direction is particularly important because curvature is
associated with changes in the tangent direction along the normal
direction.

The progression can therefore be summarized as

\[
\text{tangent}
\rightarrow
\text{normal}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{center of curvature}.
\]

This connects E15 directly with E06, E08, and E09.

### Research Significance

E15 provides an independent vector-based validation of the
differential geometry developed earlier in ASTROID-X.

Rather than studying only scalar quantities such as slope and
curvature, the experiment explicitly represents the local
orientation of the curve using tangent and normal vectors.

The machine-precision perpendicularity result also demonstrates that
the numerical implementation is consistent with the underlying
mathematical structure.
### 6.5 E16 — Algebraic Singularities
### Objective

The sixteenth experiment investigated the cusp points of the astroid
using its implicit algebraic equation.

The purpose was to verify that the four cusp points are algebraic
singularities rather than simply identifying them visually from the
parametric curve.

### Mathematical Basis

The implicit equation of the astroid is

\[
F(x,y)
=
(x^2+y^2-a^2)^3
+
27a^2x^2y^2
=
0.
\]

For an implicit curve

\[
F(x,y)=0,
\]

a singular point must satisfy both

\[
F(x,y)=0
\]

and

\[
\nabla F(x,y)=\mathbf0.
\]

The partial derivatives are

\[
F_x
=
6x(x^2+y^2-a^2)^2
+
54a^2xy^2
\]

and

\[
F_y
=
6y(x^2+y^2-a^2)^2
+
54a^2x^2y.
\]

Therefore, the singularity conditions are

\[
F(x,y)=0,
\]

\[
F_x(x,y)=0,
\]

and

\[
F_y(x,y)=0.
\]

### Expected Singular Points

The four expected cusp points are

\[
(a,0),
\qquad
(0,a),
\qquad
(-a,0),
\qquad
(0,-a).
\]

For the experimental value

\[
a=5,
\]

these become

\[
(5,0),
\qquad
(0,5),
\qquad
(-5,0),
\qquad
(0,-5).
\]

### Numerical Results

The experiment evaluated \(F\), \(F_x\), and \(F_y\) at each of the
four expected cusp points.

For all four points, the numerical results were

\[
F(x,y)=0
\]

and

\[
F_x(x,y)=F_y(x,y)=0.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\|\nabla F(x,y)\|=0
\]

at all four locations within numerical precision.

### Computational Interpretation

The result provides an algebraic verification of the cusp locations.

The same four points were previously identified through the
parametric representation and local analysis.

E16 therefore provides an independent validation using a completely
different mathematical representation of the astroid.

The small signed-zero values that may appear in floating-point output,
such as

\[
-0.000000\mathrm e+00,
\]

have no mathematical significance and represent numerical zero.

### Relationship with Earlier Experiments

The singularity has now been detected through multiple independent
approaches:

\[
\text{Parametric derivative}
\rightarrow
\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf0,
\]

\[
\text{Local expansion}
\rightarrow
y^2\sim
\frac{8}{27a}(a-x)^3,
\]

and

\[
\text{Implicit equation}
\rightarrow
F=0,\quad\nabla F=\mathbf0.
\]

These approaches describe the same cusp structure from different
mathematical perspectives.

### Research Significance

E16 strengthens the singularity analysis by showing that the cusps
are not artifacts of the chosen parametrization.

They are intrinsic singular points of the algebraic curve itself.

This completes the singularity-analysis stage of ASTROID-X and
provides a strong example of how computational experiments can
cross-validate geometric, differential, local, and algebraic
descriptions of the same mathematical object.
## 7. Generalized Family Results
### 7.1 E17 — Generalized Exponent Analysis
### Objective

The seventeenth experiment extended the analysis beyond the standard

### 8.2 Curvature, Radius of Curvature, and Evolute
### 8.2 Curvature, Radius of Curvature, and Evolute

### 8.4 Singularity Analysis Across Multiple Representations

\text{local theory}

\text{numerical estimation}

1-\frac34\sin^2(2t)
},
\]

and the local cusp scaling

\[
|y|
\sim
C(a-x)^{3/2}.
\]

These expressions were then tested numerically rather than being
treated as assumptions.

The experiments also used several forms of validation.

### Representation Validation

E01 compared different mathematical representations of the same
curve.

This established consistency between the parametric and Cartesian
descriptions.

### Numerical Validation

E10 and E11 compared numerical approximations with known analytical
perimeter and area values.

The convergence approach demonstrated that increasing the sampling
resolution improves the approximation.

### Local Validation

E12 and E13 investigated the behavior of the curve near a singular
point.

Instead of relying only on the global shape, the numerical
experiments tested the predicted local scaling law.

### Algebraic Validation

E16 used the implicit equation to independently verify the cusp
locations.

This provided a validation method that was mathematically distinct
from the parametric analysis.

### Cross-Representation Validation

The strongest methodological feature of ASTROID-X is that several
properties were investigated through more than one mathematical
representation.

For example, the cusp structure was identified through:

\[
\text{parametric derivatives},
\]

\[
\text{local asymptotic expansion},
\]

and

\[
\text{implicit algebraic singularity conditions}.
\]

Agreement between these approaches increases confidence that the
observed behavior is an intrinsic property of the astroid rather
than an artifact of a particular computational implementation.

### Reproducibility

The experiments were implemented as separate Python programs with
clearly defined parameters and mathematical objectives.

The standard scale parameter

\[
a=5
\]

was used consistently throughout the experiments unless a different
value was required by the analysis.

This makes the numerical results reproducible and allows individual
experiments to be modified or extended without changing the
mathematical framework of the entire project.

### Methodological Conclusion

The results therefore support the use of the ASTROID-X methodology
for computational mathematical research.

The project demonstrates that computational experiments are most
effective when they are connected directly to theoretical
expectations and accompanied by numerical validation.

The methodology can consequently be transferred to other curves and
geometric problems beyond the astroid.
### 9.3 Limitations of the Investigation
### 9.3 Limitations of the Investigation

Although ASTROID-X provides a broad computational investigation of the
astroid, several limitations should be recognized.

### Numerical Resolution

Many experiments rely on discretized parameter grids.

Increasing the number of sample points generally improves numerical
accuracy, but finite precision and computational cost limit the
resolution that can be practically used.

This is particularly important near the cusps, where several
quantities become very large or very small.

### Floating-Point Precision

The computational experiments use standard floating-point arithmetic.

Consequently, very small numerical differences may appear even when
two mathematical expressions are theoretically identical.

For example, E14 produced a maximum difference of approximately

\[
1.78\times10^{-15},
\]

which is consistent with floating-point precision.

Such values should therefore not be interpreted as meaningful
geometric discrepancies.

### Cusp Computations

The cusp points are singular points of the astroid.

At these locations,

\[
\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf0.
\]

Therefore, quantities that require a regular tangent vector, such as
the unit tangent and certain differential-geometric expressions,
cannot be evaluated directly at the cusp.

The experiments consequently analyze the limiting behavior near the
cusps rather than treating the cusp itself as an ordinary regular
point.

### Generalized Family

The generalized-family analysis investigated only a selected set of
exponent values.

The experiments therefore demonstrate representative behavior
rather than providing an exhaustive analysis of every possible value
of \(p\).

A more detailed parameter sweep could investigate the behavior of
geometric quantities continuously as functions of \(p\).

### Scope of Differential Analysis

The project investigates several important differential-geometric
properties, including tangent direction, curvature, radius of
curvature, and evolute behavior.

However, it does not attempt to provide a complete differential-
geometric classification of the astroid.

Additional properties could be investigated in future work.

### Scope of Numerical Methods

The numerical experiments were designed primarily for verification and
interpretation rather than for comparing multiple numerical
integration algorithms.

For example, different quadrature methods, adaptive sampling
strategies, or high-precision arithmetic could be investigated to
study numerical efficiency and error behavior in greater detail.

### Interpretation of Results

The computational results provide numerical evidence supporting the
theoretical relationships investigated in ASTROID-X.

They should therefore be interpreted as computational validation of
the mathematical framework rather than as replacements for formal
mathematical proofs.

### Overall Limitation

The main limitation of the project is therefore its scope.

ASTROID-X establishes a broad computational framework for studying the
astroid, but several directions remain open for deeper investigation.

These possibilities naturally lead to the future research directions
described in the following section.
### 9.4 Future Research Directions
### 9.4 Future Research Directions

The computational framework developed in ASTROID-X provides several
natural directions for further investigation.

### Continuous Parameter Study

The generalized family

\[
\left|\frac{x}{a}\right|^p+
\left|\frac{y}{a}\right|^p=1
\]

can be investigated over a much denser range of \(p\).

Instead of studying only selected values, future experiments could
calculate geometric quantities as continuous functions of \(p\).

Possible quantities include:

\[
A(p),
\qquad
r_{\max}(p),
\qquad
\kappa_{\min}(p),
\]

and other measurable geometric properties.

This would transform the generalized analysis into a systematic
parameter study.

### Curvature of the Generalized Family

A natural extension is to derive and numerically investigate the
curvature of the generalized curves.

This would allow the differential-geometric behavior of the astroid
to be compared directly with neighboring members of the family.

Questions could include:

\[
\text{How does curvature change as }p\text{ varies?}
\]

and

\[
\text{Which values of }p\text{ produce singular behavior?}
\]

### Singularity Transitions

The standard astroid contains four cusps.

A future investigation could study how these singularities change as
the exponent \(p\) is varied.

This would provide a connection between the generalized-family
analysis and the singularity analysis developed in E12–E16.

### Numerical Error Analysis

The current convergence experiments could be extended by comparing
different numerical methods.

Possible investigations include:

- trapezoidal integration,
- Simpson's rule,
- adaptive quadrature,
- higher-resolution polygonal approximations,
- arbitrary-precision arithmetic.

The objective would be to determine how computational method and
precision affect the measured geometric quantities.

### High-Resolution Cusp Analysis

The cusp experiments could be extended using progressively smaller
parameter neighborhoods and higher numerical precision.

This would allow the asymptotic scaling behavior to be investigated
closer to the singular point while reducing the influence of
floating-point limitations.

### Interactive Visualization

The existing experiments could eventually be integrated into an
interactive visualization system.

Parameters such as

\[
a,\quad p,\quad t
\]

could be varied dynamically while displaying corresponding changes in:

- curve geometry,
- tangent direction,
- curvature,
- radius of curvature,
- radial distance,
- singularity behavior.

Such a system would provide an experimental interface for exploring
the mathematical relationships established by ASTROID-X.

### Extension to Other Curves

The methodology developed in this project is not limited to the
astroid.

The same framework could be applied to other parametrically defined
curves, including:

- hypocycloids,
- epicycloids,
- cardioids,
- lemniscates,
- superellipses,
- other algebraic curves with singular points.

The general research pipeline would remain

\[
\boxed{
\text{Representation}

\text{Derivation}

\text{Computation}
\rightarrow
\text{Visualization}
\rightarrow
\text{Validation}
}
\]

while the mathematical properties investigated would depend on the
chosen curve.

### Research Expansion

A longer-term extension of ASTROID-X could therefore develop a
general computational geometry framework in which different curves
are analyzed using a common experimental methodology.

The astroid would serve as the initial case study, while additional
curves could be introduced to compare:

\[
\text{symmetry},
\quad
\text{curvature},
\quad
\text{singularities},
\quad
\text{area},

\quad

\]

This would transform ASTROID-X from a single-curve investigation into
a broader computational study of mathematical curves.
## 10. Final Conclusion
### Final Conclusion

ASTROID-X developed a computational framework for investigating the
astroid through connected geometric, analytical, differential, and
numerical perspectives.

The investigation began with the parametric representation

\[
x=a\cos^3(t),
\qquad
y=a\sin^3(t),
\]

which provided the foundation for the subsequent computational
analysis.

Through eighteen experiments, the project examined the astroid's
symmetry, extrema, tangent behavior, concavity, curvature, radius of
curvature, evolute, perimeter, area, cusp structure, radial behavior,
normal geometry, algebraic singularities, and generalized
superellipse-type forms.

A major result of the investigation is the consistency between these
different mathematical descriptions.

The cusp, for example, was identified through the vanishing of the
parametric derivative, described locally by the scaling law

\[
|y|\sim
\sqrt{\frac{8}{27a}}
(a-x)^{3/2},
\]

and independently verified through the implicit singularity
conditions

\[
F=0,
\qquad
\nabla F=\mathbf0.
\]

The numerical convergence experiments further demonstrated that
theoretical geometric quantities can be recovered computationally.

For the standard astroid,

\[
L=6a
\]

and

\[
A=\frac{3\pi a^2}{8},
\]

while the generalized-family experiments demonstrated how geometric
properties change when the exponent \(p\) is varied.

The most important outcome of ASTROID-X is therefore not a single
formula or numerical value.

It is the development of a reproducible methodology:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Theory}
\rightarrow
\text{Derivation}
\rightarrow
\text{Computation}
\rightarrow
\text{Visualization}
\rightarrow
\text{Validation}
\rightarrow
\text{Interpretation}
}
\]

This methodology allows mathematical properties to be investigated
computationally while maintaining a direct connection with their
theoretical foundations.

The project also demonstrates the value of using multiple
representations of the same mathematical object. Parametric,
Cartesian, polar, and implicit descriptions provide different
computational perspectives, while agreement between them strengthens
the interpretation of the resulting observations.

Finally, the generalized exponent study shows that the framework can
extend beyond the classical astroid. The same computational approach
can be applied to broader families of curves and, ultimately, to
other problems in computational geometry.

Therefore, ASTROID-X can be regarded as a computational case study of
how mathematical theory can be transformed into reproducible
experiments, numerical evidence, graphical analysis, and
research-oriented interpretation.
